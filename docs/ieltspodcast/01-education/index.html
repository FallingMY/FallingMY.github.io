    What Is Education Really For?

    October 22, 202617 min read IELTS IELTS PodcastEducationIELTS Writing

    Contents

    An IELTS Writing Podcast on Education · 时长 23:55 · 3,461 words

    Hello, everyone. Welcome back to the podcast. Today, I want to talk about something that sounds incredibly ordinary, but if you think about it for a few minutes, it becomes surprisingly complicated.

    And that is education. Now, if you’re preparing for IELTS Writing Task 2, you have probably already seen a huge number of questions about education. Should university education be free?

    Should students be allowed to choose whatever subjects they want? Is it better to study at university or start working immediately? Should schools focus more on practical skills?

    Are examinations an effective way of assessing students? Should parents or teachers be mainly responsible for children’s education? And here’s another very common type of question:

    What is the main purpose of education? Is it to prepare young people for employment? Is it to help them become responsible citizens?

    Is it to develop their personality? Or is it simply to provide them with knowledge? These questions look different on the surface, but actually, they are often asking us to discuss the same underlying issue:

    What is education really for? And today, rather than giving you a list of vocabulary to memorize, I want to have a conversation about this topic. Because when you understand the ideas behind the language, the language becomes much easier to use.

    Education is more than getting a job

    Let’s start with a very common argument. Some people believe that the primary purpose of education is to prepare young people for the job market. And, honestly, I can understand where this argument comes from.

    We spend years at school. Then we go to university. We study for exams.

    We get qualifications. And eventually, we enter the workforce. So it’s reasonable to ask:

    What is all of this education supposed to prepare us for? Well, one answer is employment. Schools and universities can equip students with knowledge and skills that are useful in the workplace.

    For example, students might learn mathematics, computer science, engineering, economics, or foreign languages. These subjects can give young people the knowledge they need to pursue a particular career. And there’s a useful expression here:

    equip somebody with something This is a very useful academic expression. You can say:

    Education equips students with the knowledge and skills they need to succeed in the workplace. Notice that I didn’t say: “Education gives students many skills.”

    That’s grammatically correct, but equip somebody with something sounds much more natural in formal writing. You can also say: Schools should equip young people with practical skills.

    Or: Higher education should equip graduates with the skills required by the modern economy. This expression isn’t limited to education, either.

    A good education can equip people with the tools they need to deal with the challenges of adult life. That’s a much broader idea. And this brings us to an important point.

    Education may prepare people for employment, but perhaps it shouldn’t be limited to employment. That’s another expression I want you to notice: be limited to something

    For example: > The purpose of education should not be limited to preparing students for employment. That’s an excellent sentence for IELTS.

    Why? Because you’ve immediately introduced a contrast. You’re basically saying:

    “Yes, employment matters, but education has a wider purpose.” And now we’re getting into a much more interesting argument.

    Education and the development of individuals

    Imagine a student who gets excellent grades. They get into a good university. They graduate with a degree.

    They get a well-paid job. Sounds great, right? But imagine that this person cannot communicate effectively.

    They cannot work with other people. They don’t know how to solve problems independently. They have never learned how to think critically.

    They are completely dependent on other people when they face an unfamiliar situation. Would we still say that education has been successful? I think this is where the idea of holistic development becomes important.

    “Holistic” is a useful word in education essays. It basically means looking at the whole person rather than focusing on one particular aspect. So you might say:

    Education should promote the holistic development of young people. Or: > Schools should focus not only on academic achievement but also on the holistic development of students.

    That sentence contains another extremely useful structure: not only… but also… Now, I know what you’re thinking.

    “Come on. That’s a basic structure.” And you’re right. It is basic.

    But basic does not mean useless. In fact, one of the biggest mistakes IELTS students make is thinking that a high-band essay needs to be full of complicated grammar. It doesn’t.

    What matters is whether your language is accurate, natural and appropriately used. A simple structure used naturally is much better than an extremely complicated structure that you can’t control. So:

    Schools should focus not only on academic achievement but also on students’ personal development. That’s perfectly good. You don’t need to write something like:

    It is of paramount importance for educational institutions to facilitate the multifaceted advancement of individuals… Please don’t. It sounds like you’re trying to impress the examiner rather than communicate an idea.

    And IELTS is a language test. Not a competition to see who can sound the most pretentious.

    What about exams?

    Now let’s talk about something that almost every student has an opinion about: examinations. Are exams useful?

    Well, yes. And no. It depends on what we expect exams to do.

    One advantage of examinations is that they provide a relatively standardized way of assessing students. If thousands of students take the same test under similar conditions, universities and employers can compare their performance. So exams can provide a standardized means of assessment.

    That’s a very useful phrase. You could write: > Examinations provide a standardized means of assessing students’ academic performance.

    But there is a problem. If students know that exams are the most important thing in their education, they may start focusing almost entirely on exam performance. And when that happens, education can become overly focused on memorization.

    Students may ask themselves: “What do I need to remember for the test?” rather than:

    “How can I understand this?” And those are very different questions. This is where the expression place too much emphasis on becomes useful.

    For example: > Some education systems place too much emphasis on examination results. That’s a very useful IELTS sentence.

    You can use it in many different contexts. Governments may place too much emphasis on economic growth. Parents may place too much emphasis on academic achievement.

    Companies may place too much emphasis on short-term profits. Schools may place too much emphasis on standardized testing. So if you remember one structure from today’s episode, remember:

    place too much emphasis on something. It is simple, flexible, and extremely useful.

    But are exams really the problem?

    Now, let’s be fair. It’s easy to criticize examinations. We can say:

    “Exams are stressful.” “Exams encourage memorization.” “Exams don’t measure creativity.”

    All of these arguments may have some truth to them. But that doesn’t necessarily mean we should get rid of exams completely. Why?

    Because every assessment system has limitations. If we replace written examinations with group projects, for example, we create a different set of problems. How do we know whether one student did most of the work?

    How do we compare students fairly? What happens if one student has much better communication skills than another? So instead of saying:

    Exams are completely useless. A more sophisticated argument would be: > Although examinations are not a perfect method of assessment, they can still provide a relatively objective way of comparing students’ academic performance.

    Notice the language. not a perfect method rather than

    completely useless This is what we call qualified language. You’re not presenting everything as black and white.

    You’re saying: “There’s a problem, but there’s also a reason why this system exists.” And this is incredibly useful in IELTS Task 2.

    Because real-world issues are rarely completely black and white.

    A useful IELTS habit: don’t think in extremes

    Let’s stop for a second. This is actually one of the most important habits I want you to develop. When you see a question like:

    Some people believe that examinations are the best way to assess students. Others think that examinations should be replaced by other forms of assessment. Discuss both views and give your own opinion. Don’t immediately think: “Which side should I choose?”

    Instead, ask yourself: Why might each side be reasonable? That’s a much better question.

    If you can understand both sides, your writing becomes much easier. For example: Exams are useful because they are standardized.

    But they may encourage memorization. Group projects can assess communication and teamwork. But they can be difficult to assess fairly.

    Practical assessments can measure real-world skills. But they may be more expensive and time-consuming. See what happened?

    We didn’t memorize any complicated vocabulary. We simply considered the issue from different perspectives. And once you have the ideas, the language follows.

    The role of schools

    Let’s move on. What should schools actually teach? Traditional subjects?

    Mathematics? Science? History?

    Literature? Or practical life skills? Imagine a teenager who knows how to solve complicated mathematical equations but doesn’t know how to manage money.

    They can analyze Shakespeare but have no idea how to write a CV. They can memorize historical dates but don’t know how to communicate effectively with colleagues. Would that be a good education?

    Again, I don’t think the answer is simply yes or no. Academic knowledge is valuable. But practical skills are valuable too.

    So perhaps schools should strike a balance between academic knowledge and practical skills. And there we have another excellent IELTS expression: strike a balance between A and B

    For example: > Schools need to strike a balance between academic knowledge and practical skills. You can use it everywhere.

    Governments need to strike a balance between economic development and environmental protection. Parents need to strike a balance between discipline and freedom. People need to strike a balance between work and personal life.

    Technology companies need to strike a balance between innovation and privacy. It’s one of those expressions that is worth learning as a chunk. Don’t learn:

    “strike” then “a”

    then “balance” then

    “between.” Learn the whole thing: strike a balance between A and B.

    That’s how natural language works. You store useful pieces. Then you pull them out when you need them.

    Academic knowledge still matters

    However, let’s not go too far in the other direction. Sometimes when people talk about practical education, they make it sound as though academic knowledge doesn’t matter anymore. I disagree.

    Imagine studying medicine without studying biology. Imagine becoming an engineer without learning mathematics. Imagine becoming a lawyer without studying law.

    Obviously, specialized knowledge matters. So the argument shouldn’t be: “Academic knowledge is useless.”

    It should be: > Academic knowledge should be complemented by practical skills. And be complemented by is another beautiful phrase.

    For example: > Theoretical knowledge should be complemented by practical experience. That’s an excellent sentence.

    You can use this idea in university education. You can use it in professional training. You can even use it in language learning.

    Learning grammar should be complemented by opportunities to communicate in real situations. Learning vocabulary should be complemented by regular speaking and writing practice. And, actually, that’s one of the reasons I’m doing this podcast.

    Because knowing a word is one thing. Being able to use it naturally is something else.

    What about critical thinking?

    Now let’s talk about another phrase that appears again and again in education discussions: critical thinking. Critical thinking means being able to examine information carefully, question assumptions, compare different arguments, and form your own conclusions.

    And I think this is becoming increasingly important. Why? Because modern students have access to an enormous amount of information.

    They can search for almost anything online. They can watch lectures. They can read articles.

    They can ask AI. They can watch videos. They can access information from people all over the world.

    So perhaps the challenge is no longer simply: “How can I find information?” The challenge is:

    “How can I decide whether this information is reliable?” And that’s a completely different skill. This is why you might say:

    Education should encourage students to think critically rather than simply memorize information. Notice the contrast: rather than simply memorize information

    This is another structure worth absorbing. You could say: > Teachers should encourage students to solve problems independently rather than simply provide them with answers.

    Or: > Schools should encourage students to develop their own opinions rather than simply accept information without questioning it. These are very natural academic sentences.

    Should teachers give students all the answers?

    Here’s an interesting question. Suppose a student asks a teacher: “I don’t understand this problem. Can you tell me the answer?”

    What should the teacher do? Give them the answer? Or help them find it themselves?

    I think there’s value in both approaches. If the student has absolutely no idea what to do, giving them some guidance is necessary. But if teachers always provide the answers, students may become dependent on them.

    A good teacher should sometimes guide students towards the answer rather than simply give it to them. And that expression— guide somebody towards something

    —is another useful one. For example: > Teachers should guide students towards developing independent learning skills.

    Notice that we’re building an entire vocabulary network here. We’re not learning isolated words. Education:

    equip students with skills promote holistic development place emphasis on academic achievement

    strike a balance between theory and practice complement academic knowledge with practical experience encourage critical thinking

    develop independent learning skills guide students towards solutions These expressions are connected.

    And that’s exactly what makes them easier to remember.

    Is education the responsibility of schools?

    Now let’s move outside the classroom. Who is responsible for a child’s education? Teachers?

    Parents? The government? Society?

    This is another classic IELTS question. And, again, I don’t think we should choose only one. Parents play an important role because children spend a huge amount of time at home.

    Parents influence children’s habits, attitudes and behavior. Schools, on the other hand, provide structured education and professional instruction. Governments establish educational policies and provide funding.

    So perhaps education should be viewed as a shared responsibility. That’s a very useful phrase. a shared responsibility

    You could say: > Educating children should be regarded as a shared responsibility between parents, schools and governments. That’s a strong sentence.

    And there’s another phrase here: play a crucial role in For example:

    Parents play a crucial role in shaping children’s attitudes towards education. Or: > Schools play a crucial role in developing students’ social and communication skills.

    Or: > Governments play a crucial role in ensuring equal access to education. Again, nothing complicated.

    But these are highly reusable structures.

    What does “equal access to education” actually mean?

    Let’s think about government responsibility for a moment. Imagine two children. One grows up in a wealthy city.

    Their parents can afford private tutors. They have a laptop. They have a quiet room to study in.

    They can attend expensive language classes. They have access to books and educational resources. Another child grows up in a remote area.

    Their school has fewer teachers. They have limited access to technology. Their parents cannot afford extra classes.

    Now imagine that both students take the same exam. Can we really say that they have had the same educational opportunities? Probably not.

    And that’s where the phrase equal access to education becomes important. Governments may need to ensure that children from different social and economic backgrounds have access to basic educational resources. This leads us to another very useful phrase:

    regardless of For example: > Every child should have access to quality education regardless of their family background.

    That’s an excellent IELTS sentence. You can use regardless of in many contexts. People should have access to healthcare regardless of their income.

    Employees should be treated fairly regardless of their age. Students should have opportunities to learn regardless of where they live. It’s a small phrase, but it’s incredibly powerful.

    Education and social inequality

    And now we’ve arrived at a much bigger issue. Education doesn’t exist in isolation. It is connected to society.

    If wealthy families can provide their children with better educational opportunities, education may actually reinforce existing social inequalities. In other words, instead of reducing inequality, the education system might sometimes perpetuate inequality. “Perpetuate” is a more advanced word, and I wouldn’t tell you to use it simply because it sounds advanced.

    But if you understand it, it’s useful. It means to make something continue for a long time. So:

    An unequal education system may perpetuate social inequality. That’s a strong sentence. But remember: don’t use “perpetuate” if you’re not comfortable with it.

    You could simply say: > An unequal education system may make existing social inequalities worse. That’s completely fine.

    And actually, that’s an important principle for IELTS: precision is more important than sophistication. If you know a simple word that expresses exactly what you mean, use it.

    So what should education ultimately achieve?

    Let’s come back to our original question. What is education really for? Maybe it’s not about choosing between employment, knowledge, personal development and citizenship.

    Maybe good education should do several things at the same time. It should provide knowledge. It should develop practical skills.

    It should prepare young people for employment. It should encourage critical thinking. It should help people communicate with others.

    It should develop independence. And perhaps most importantly, it should give people the ability to continue learning throughout their lives. And that brings us to a beautiful concept:

    lifelong learning. Lifelong learning means that education doesn’t stop when you graduate. The world changes.

    Technology changes. Industries change. The skills people need change.

    So people need to continue learning. You could say: > In a rapidly changing world, lifelong learning is becoming increasingly important.

    That’s a very IELTS-friendly sentence. And you can connect it to technology: > As technology continues to transform the workplace, individuals need to update their skills throughout their careers.

    Look at that phrase: update their skills Very useful.

    You can also say: acquire new skills develop new skills

    adapt to changing demands keep up with technological developments These expressions can be used not only in education essays but also in technology, employment and globalization topics.

    And this is the real secret of IELTS vocabulary

    Now, before we finish, I want to make one thing very clear. If you’re preparing for IELTS Writing Task 2, don’t think: “I need 500 advanced words.”

    That’s not really the goal. Instead, build what I would call a network of useful expressions. For today’s topic, for example, you might have:

    equip students with knowledge and skills prepare young people for employment promote holistic development

    place too much emphasis on examinations provide a standardized means of assessment strike a balance between theory and practice

    complement academic knowledge with practical experience encourage critical thinking develop independent learning skills

    play a crucial role in education ensure equal access to education regardless of family background

    perpetuate social inequality promote lifelong learning adapt to changing demands

    That’s already a powerful vocabulary set. But don’t memorize these as fifteen separate dictionary entries. Think about the ideas connecting them.

    Education provides knowledge. Knowledge needs to be complemented by practical skills. Students need to think independently.

    Exams can assess knowledge but may encourage memorization. Schools need to strike a balance. Governments need to ensure equal access.

    And education should prepare people not just for their first job, but for a lifetime of learning. Once you understand that whole chain of ideas, you don’t need to force vocabulary into your essay. The expressions will naturally become available when you need them.

    One final thought

    Let me leave you with one question. Imagine a student who gets perfect scores in every examination. They remember enormous amounts of information.

    They get into a prestigious university. But they are unable to think independently. They cannot communicate with other people.

    They cannot adapt when circumstances change. They don’t know how to learn by themselves. Would we call that person well educated?

    I’m not sure. And perhaps that’s the point. Education isn’t simply about filling someone’s head with information.

    It is also about giving people the ability to use knowledge, question information, solve problems, work with others, and continue learning. So when you see an IELTS question about education, don’t immediately search your memory for a “Band 9 vocabulary list.” First, ask yourself:

    What kind of person should education help us become? Once you have an answer to that question, the essay becomes much easier. And more importantly, your English becomes much more natural.

    Because you’re no longer trying to decorate your ideas with complicated words. You’re using English to express ideas that you actually understand. And that is ultimately what good IELTS writing is about.

    Thanks for listening. I’ll see you in the next episode.
