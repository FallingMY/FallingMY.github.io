    Is Technology Making Our Lives Better?

    October 22, 202622 min read IELTS IELTS PodcastIELTS WritingTechnology

    Contents

    An IELTS Writing Podcast on Technology · 时长 31:45 · 4,526 words

    Hello everyone, and welcome back to the podcast. Today, we’re going to talk about one of the most common topics in IELTS Writing Task 2: technology.

    And if you’ve taken IELTS before, you probably already know that “technology” is one of those topics that seems to appear everywhere. Technology and education. Technology and employment.

    Technology and communication. Technology and children. Artificial intelligence.

    Social media. Privacy. The Internet.

    Automation. And, of course, the classic question: Has technology made our lives better or worse?

    Now, at first glance, that sounds like a very simple question. Obviously, technology has made our lives better, right? We have smartphones.

    We have high-speed Internet. We can communicate with people on the other side of the world. We can order food from our phones.

    We can work from home. We can learn almost anything online. But then again, technology has created new problems.

    People spend hours scrolling through social media. Children spend less time playing outside. Some jobs are being replaced by machines.

    Companies collect enormous amounts of personal data. And now, with artificial intelligence becoming more powerful, we have a whole new set of questions. So today, instead of asking whether technology is simply “good” or “bad”, let’s take a more balanced approach.

    Because that’s exactly the kind of thinking that can help you in IELTS Writing Task 2.

    Technology has transformed everyday life

    Let’s start with something obvious. Technology has transformed the way we live. Notice that expression:

    transform the way we live That’s much more natural than constantly saying: “Technology changed our lives.”

    “Transform” suggests a much more significant change. You could say: > Technological advances have transformed the way people work, communicate and access information.

    That’s a very useful sentence. And notice another phrase: technological advances

    You will see this phrase everywhere in discussions about technology. It simply means developments or improvements in technology. For example:

    Technological advances have made it possible for people to work remotely. Or: > Rapid technological advances have created new opportunities for businesses.

    Or: > Technological advances in medicine have significantly improved people’s quality of life. You don’t need to memorize these sentences word for word.

    Just remember the chunk: technological advances have… And then you can complete it according to the question.

    Technology saves us time

    Think about your daily life. How many things do you do without technology? Probably not many.

    You wake up and check your phone. You use an app to check the weather. You order something online.

    You use navigation to find somewhere. You communicate with friends through messaging apps. You pay for things electronically.

    You watch videos. You listen to music. You study online.

    And all of these technologies have one thing in common: They save time. And that’s another very useful expression:

    save time and effort Technology can save people enormous amounts of time and effort. For example:

    Online banking allows people to manage their finances without having to visit a bank in person. That sentence gives you another useful structure: without having to do something

    You could say: > Online shopping allows consumers to purchase products without having to visit physical stores. Or:

    Online education enables students to access educational resources without having to attend traditional classrooms. Notice the verb: enable

    This is an extremely useful verb in IELTS. Technology enables people to do something. Not:

    “Technology makes people can do something.” That’s a common mistake. You should say:

    Technology enables people to access information more easily. Or: > The Internet enables students to learn from experts around the world.

    So remember the structure: enable somebody to do something It’s one of those grammar-and-vocabulary combinations that is worth learning as a single chunk.

    The Internet has changed access to information

    Now let’s talk about information. This might actually be one of the biggest changes technology has brought about. In the past, if you wanted to learn something, you might have had to go to a library.

    You might have needed to buy a book. You might have needed to find an expert. Today?

    You can simply search for it. And that’s amazing. The Internet has made information more accessible.

    There’s a phrase worth remembering: make something more accessible For example:

    The Internet has made educational resources more accessible to people around the world. Or: > Digital technology has made healthcare information more accessible to the general public.

    “Accessible” here basically means easier to obtain or use. And there’s an important distinction. You don’t necessarily need to say:

    “Technology gives people more information.” That’s true, but it’s not particularly precise. You can say:

    Technology has made information more readily accessible. That’s a much more academic way of expressing the same basic idea.

    But more information doesn’t necessarily mean better information

    Here’s where things become interesting. The Internet gives us access to more information than ever before. But does that mean we are better informed?

    Not necessarily. Because the Internet also contains misinformation. Anyone can publish something online.

    A person doesn’t need to be an expert to create a video. They don’t need to be a journalist to write an article. They don’t need to have any qualifications to express an opinion.

    So we’re facing a new problem. The problem isn’t simply: How do we access information?

    It’s: How do we evaluate information? And this brings us back to something we talked about in our education episode:

    critical thinking. People need to be able to distinguish between reliable and unreliable sources. They need to question information rather than automatically believe everything they see online.

    So you could write: > Although the Internet provides unprecedented access to information, users still need to develop the ability to distinguish between reliable and unreliable sources. That is a very strong IELTS sentence.

    And look at the phrase: distinguish between A and B This is extremely useful.

    You can distinguish between: reliable and unreliable information, fact and opinion,

    short-term and long-term consequences, essential and unnecessary spending, or genuine and misleading advertising.

    It’s another excellent chunk.

    Technology and education

    Now let’s move into one of the most common IELTS combinations: technology and education. Should technology play a greater role in education?

    I would say yes. But again, there is a “but.” Technology can provide students with access to educational materials that were previously unavailable to them.

    A student living in a small town can watch a lecture from a professor at a major university. Someone learning English can communicate with people from other countries. A student struggling with mathematics can find hundreds of explanations online.

    This means technology can expand access to education. That’s a very useful expression. > Digital technology can expand access to education, particularly for people living in remote areas.

    But there is another side. Technology can also become a distraction. Imagine a student trying to study while receiving notifications every two minutes.

    Message. TikTok. Instagram.

    YouTube. Another message. Another notification.

    It becomes difficult to concentrate. So the question isn’t necessarily whether technology should be used in education. The better question is:

    How should technology be used? That’s a much more sophisticated way of approaching the issue.

    Technology should complement, not replace, teachers

    Here’s my personal view. I don’t think technology should completely replace teachers. Why?

    Because education isn’t simply about transferring information. A teacher can understand a student’s difficulties. A teacher can notice when a student is confused.

    A teacher can encourage a student who has lost motivation. A teacher can adapt their explanation to the individual learner. Technology can help with some of these things.

    But human interaction still matters. So we could say: > Technology should complement traditional teaching methods rather than replace teachers entirely.

    And there are two excellent expressions here. First: complement something

    Second: rather than This is a very useful way to make a balanced argument.

    You don’t have to say: “Technology is good.” You can say:

    Technology should be used to complement traditional teaching methods rather than replace them. That’s a much more nuanced position. And “complement” is particularly useful because it means that two things work well together.

    For example: > Online learning can complement face-to-face instruction. Or:

    Practical training should complement theoretical knowledge.

    What about online education?

    Let’s imagine that you have two options. You can attend a traditional university. Or you can study almost entirely online.

    Which is better? Well, online education has obvious advantages. It’s flexible.

    You don’t necessarily have to move to another city. You can study at your own pace. You can watch lectures again.

    You can access materials whenever you want. So online education can provide greater flexibility. That’s another phrase worth remembering:

    provide greater flexibility For example: > Online courses provide students with greater flexibility in terms of when and where they study.

    But face-to-face education has advantages too. Students can interact with teachers directly. They can work with classmates.

    They can build social connections. They can participate in discussions. So once again, we don’t necessarily have to choose one side.

    Perhaps the best approach is a combination of the two. And we have another useful expression: a combination of A and B

    A combination of online and face-to-face learning may provide the best educational experience. Simple. Natural.

    Useful.

    Technology and communication

    Now let’s move away from education. Technology has completely transformed the way we communicate. Think about it.

    Twenty years ago, if you wanted to talk to someone who lived abroad, you might have made an expensive phone call. Today, you can have a video call for almost nothing. You can send photographs instantly.

    You can share your location. You can send voice messages. You can communicate with dozens of people at the same time.

    So technology has removed many geographical barriers. That’s an excellent phrase. > Digital communication has removed many geographical barriers and made it easier for people to maintain relationships across borders.

    But—and this is important—communication has become easier, but has it become better? That’s debatable. We can communicate with more people.

    But sometimes our communication becomes less meaningful. Think about texting. You can send someone:

    “How are you?” They reply: “Good.”

    And that’s it. Compare that with sitting down with a friend and having a two-hour conversation. They’re not necessarily the same experience.

    So technology may have increased the quantity of communication, but not always the quality of communication. And these two words—quantity and quality—are very useful when you’re discussing social issues. You can say:

    Technology has increased the quantity of communication, but this does not necessarily mean that the quality of human interaction has improved. That’s a strong argument.

    Social media

    And now we have to talk about social media. Social media has probably become one of the biggest sources of debate around technology. On the positive side, social media allows people to stay connected.

    You can keep in touch with old friends. You can build communities around shared interests. Small businesses can reach potential customers.

    People can share ideas and information. And marginalized groups can find communities that may not exist in their local environment. So social media can facilitate communication.

    That’s a very useful verb: facilitate It means to make something easier.

    Technology facilitates communication. The Internet facilitates access to information. Public transport facilitates mobility.

    Education facilitates social mobility. So you could write: > Social media facilitates communication between people who live in different parts of the world.

    But again, there are drawbacks.

    The problem of excessive screen time

    One major concern is excessive screen time. People can spend hours scrolling through social media. And sometimes, they’re not even enjoying it.

    They just keep scrolling. Another video. Another post.

    Another video. And suddenly an hour has disappeared. This can have a negative impact on people’s productivity.

    You can say: > Excessive screen time can have a negative impact on people’s productivity and well-being. Notice this phrase:

    have a negative impact on Very common. You can also say:

    have a positive impact on For example: > Technology can have a positive impact on students’ learning.

    So instead of always saying: “Technology is good for…” or

    “Technology is bad for…” use: have a positive/negative impact on something.

    It sounds much more natural in academic writing.

    Technology and mental well-being

    Another concern is mental well-being. Social media platforms often encourage people to compare themselves with others. You see someone else’s holiday.

    Someone else’s expensive car. Someone else’s perfect body. Someone else’s successful career.

    And you start thinking: “Why isn’t my life like that?” Of course, we know that social media often presents an unrealistic picture of people’s lives.

    But emotionally, knowing that doesn’t always stop us from comparing ourselves with others. So you could argue: > Excessive use of social media may contribute to anxiety and low self-esteem, particularly among young people.

    The key phrase here is: contribute to Notice that we’re not saying:

    “Social media causes depression.” That’s an extremely strong claim. Instead:

    Social media may contribute to mental health problems. That’s more cautious. And cautious language is extremely important in academic writing.

    You can use: may might

    can is likely to could potentially

    These expressions help you avoid making claims that are unnecessarily absolute.

    Children and technology

    Now let’s talk about children. This is another classic IELTS topic. Should children have unlimited access to technology?

    Probably not. Children can benefit enormously from technology. Educational apps can make learning more interactive.

    Online resources can encourage curiosity. Technology can help children develop digital skills. But excessive use can create problems.

    Children may spend less time outdoors. They may interact less with other children face-to-face. They may become dependent on digital entertainment.

    And excessive screen time may affect sleep. So perhaps parents should set reasonable limits on children’s screen time. That is a very useful phrase.

    set limits on something Parents can set limits on screen time. Governments can set limits on advertising.

    Schools can set limits on phone use. You can also say: > Parents should encourage children to develop healthy digital habits.

    That’s another nice expression: develop healthy habits Not only digital habits.

    People can develop healthy eating habits. Healthy sleeping habits. Healthy exercise habits.

    Healthy study habits. Again, one expression gives you lots of possibilities.

    Technology and employment

    Now let’s move to one of the biggest technology questions: Will technology take our jobs? You’ve probably heard this question many times.

    Artificial intelligence. Robots. Automation.

    Machine learning. People worry that machines will replace human workers. And this isn’t completely unreasonable.

    Machines can already perform many repetitive tasks faster and more accurately than humans. For example, factories can use robots for assembly. Companies can automate customer service.

    Software can process enormous amounts of data. AI can generate text, images and code. So technological automation may displace certain types of jobs.

    “Displace” is a useful word here. It means that something replaces something else. You could say:

    Automation may displace workers who perform repetitive tasks. But here’s an important point. Technology doesn’t necessarily eliminate work altogether.

    It often changes the nature of work. Think about the history of technology. When cars became popular, some jobs disappeared.

    But new jobs appeared. When computers entered offices, some tasks became automated. But new industries emerged.

    So perhaps the better question isn’t: “Will technology destroy jobs?” It’s:

    What kinds of jobs will exist in the future? That’s a much more interesting question.

    Technology creates new employment opportunities

    Technology doesn’t only eliminate jobs. It also creates new employment opportunities. Think about jobs that barely existed twenty years ago.

    App developers. Social media managers. Data scientists.

    AI engineers. Content creators. Cybersecurity specialists.

    And countless others. So we could say: > Technological innovation can create new employment opportunities while making some existing occupations less necessary.

    That’s a very balanced sentence. Notice the structure: while + contrasting idea

    It allows you to acknowledge two sides in one sentence. You don’t need to write: “Technology creates jobs.”

    Then: “Technology destroys jobs.” Then:

    “This is complicated.” You can combine the ideas: > Technological innovation can create new employment opportunities while making some existing occupations less necessary.

    That’s concise. And that’s something you should aim for in IELTS. Not long sentences for the sake of being long.

    Dense sentences that communicate a clear relationship between ideas.

    Workers need to adapt

    If technology changes the job market, what should workers do? They need to adapt. This gives us another extremely useful phrase:

    adapt to changing circumstances For example: > Workers need to adapt to changing technological and economic circumstances.

    Or: > Employees may need to acquire new skills in order to adapt to changes in the labour market. And now we have another useful structure:

    in order to Again, this isn’t advanced grammar. But it’s useful.

    You can say: > People need to acquire new skills in order to remain competitive in the labour market. That’s much better than trying to force some complicated grammatical structure into your essay.

    The importance of lifelong learning—again

    And here we return to an idea from our education episode: lifelong learning. If technology changes rapidly, education cannot stop when people graduate.

    Workers may need to retrain. They may need to learn new software. They may need to develop new skills.

    They may even need to change careers. This is sometimes called reskilling or upskilling. You don’t have to use these words if you’re not comfortable with them.

    You can simply say: > Workers may need to learn new skills throughout their careers. Perfectly clear.

    And actually, clarity is more important than vocabulary difficulty.

    What about privacy?

    Now let’s move into a different area. Technology gives us convenience. But convenience often comes at a cost.

    Think about how much information companies can collect about us. Where we go. What we search for.

    What we buy. What we watch. Who we communicate with.

    What we are interested in. And sometimes, we don’t even realize how much information we’re giving away. So privacy has become a major concern.

    You could say: > The widespread use of digital technology has raised concerns about data privacy. That’s an excellent phrase:

    raise concerns about something It means to make people worried about something. For example:

    The increasing use of AI has raised concerns about job security. > Social media has raised concerns about children’s mental health. > Online shopping has raised concerns about consumer privacy.

    Again, one chunk, many applications.

    Personal data

    Let’s think about personal data. Companies may collect information about users in order to provide personalized services. For example, if an online store knows what you’ve searched for, it can recommend products you might like.

    That’s convenient. But where should we draw the line? At what point does personalization become surveillance?

    That’s a difficult question. And this is where governments may need to regulate technology companies. You can use the verb:

    regulate Governments regulate industries. They regulate financial markets.

    They regulate food safety. And increasingly, governments are trying to regulate digital platforms and artificial intelligence. A useful sentence would be:

    Governments need to establish appropriate regulations to protect consumers’ personal data. Notice: establish regulations

    and protect personal data Both are very useful combinations.

    Should governments regulate artificial intelligence?

    Now let’s talk about AI. This is becoming increasingly relevant to IELTS-style discussions. AI can potentially improve productivity.

    It can automate repetitive tasks. It can assist doctors. It can help students learn.

    It can translate languages. It can analyze large amounts of information. It can help companies make decisions.

    So there are enormous potential benefits. But there are also risks. AI systems can make mistakes.

    They can reproduce biases in their training data. They can be misused. They can affect employment.

    They can generate misleading information. And nobody completely knows what increasingly powerful AI systems will mean for society in the long term. So should governments regulate AI?

    I would say some level of regulation is necessary. But governments also need to avoid stifling innovation. “Stifle” means to prevent something from developing.

    So: > Excessive regulation could stifle technological innovation. That’s a very nice advanced expression.

    But again, don’t use it just because it sounds advanced. If you don’t remember “stifle”, simply say: > Excessive regulation could slow down technological development.

    That’s absolutely fine.

    Regulation versus innovation

    And here we have another classic IELTS balancing problem. On one side: We need regulation to protect people.

    On the other: Too much regulation could slow innovation. So perhaps governments need to strike a balance between innovation and regulation.

    There it is again: strike a balance between A and B. This is why I keep repeating certain expressions.

    Because in IELTS, you don’t need hundreds of unrelated phrases. You need a relatively small number of flexible structures that you can use accurately across different topics.

    Technology and inequality

    Here’s another issue that is often overlooked. Technology can create opportunities. But not everyone has equal access to technology.

    Imagine a student with a fast Internet connection, a laptop, a quiet room and access to high-quality online courses. Now imagine another student who has only a cheap smartphone and an unstable Internet connection. Both technically have “access to the Internet.”

    But their opportunities are clearly different. This is sometimes described as the digital divide. The digital divide refers to the gap between people who have good access to digital technology and those who don’t.

    So technological progress does not automatically mean social progress. That’s an important sentence: > Technological progress does not necessarily lead to greater social equality.

    And this is a very useful pattern: X does not necessarily lead to Y. Economic growth does not necessarily lead to greater equality.

    More information does not necessarily lead to better decisions. Higher salaries do not necessarily lead to greater happiness. Technological progress does not necessarily lead to social progress.

    It’s an incredibly flexible structure.

    So, is technology good or bad?

    Let’s come back to the original question. Has technology made our lives better or worse? If you want my answer, I’d say:

    Both. Technology itself isn’t really the issue. What matters is how we use it and how society manages it.

    A smartphone can help you learn a language. The same smartphone can help you waste three hours scrolling through videos. AI can help a student understand a difficult concept.

    The same technology can be used to spread misinformation. Social media can help someone find a supportive community. It can also make people feel inadequate.

    Automation can eliminate certain jobs. It can also create new ones. So rather than asking whether technology is inherently good or bad, we should ask:

    How can we maximize its benefits while minimizing its potential harm? And there is a beautiful IELTS structure here: maximize the benefits while minimizing the risks.

    For example: > Governments should develop policies that maximize the benefits of AI while minimizing the risks associated with its misuse. That’s an excellent sentence.

    You can use it for almost any technology question.

    A very useful way to think about technology essays

    So, if you get a technology question in IELTS, I want you to think about four things. First: Benefits.

    What does technology make easier? Does it save time? Does it reduce costs?

    Does it improve access? Does it increase productivity? Does it create opportunities?

    Second: Risks. Could it create unemployment?

    Could it reduce privacy? Could it increase inequality? Could it affect mental health?

    Could it encourage dependence? Third: Human behavior.

    Technology itself doesn’t make every decision. People decide how to use it. Parents decide how much screen time children get.

    Companies decide how they use personal data. Individuals decide how much time they spend online. Fourth:

    Government responsibility. Should governments regulate something? Should they provide access?

    Should they protect vulnerable groups? Should they invest in digital infrastructure? Should they establish regulations?

    Once you think in these four directions, you’ll find it much easier to generate ideas.

    The vocabulary network

    Let’s quickly review the expressions we’ve encountered today. Not as a vocabulary list. Think of them as tools.

    Technology can: transform the way we live save time and effort

    enable people to do something make information more accessible expand access to education

    facilitate communication remove geographical barriers have a positive or negative impact on something

    contribute to a problem raise concerns about something create employment opportunities

    displace workers require people to adapt protect personal data

    stifle innovation increase productivity And governments can:

    establish regulations regulate technology companies protect consumers

    reduce inequality ensure equal access And when two things have to coexist, we can:

    strike a balance between A and B complement A with B maximize the benefits while minimizing the risks

    Those three structures alone can take you surprisingly far.

    Don’t turn your IELTS essay into a technology advertisement

    There’s one final mistake I want you to avoid. Sometimes students write technology essays like this: “Technology is very important.

    Technology makes our lives easier. Technology makes our lives faster. Technology makes our lives better.

    Technology is convenient. Technology is useful.” Okay.

    But what’s actually happening here? You’re making the same point six times. Instead, ask:

    How? How does technology make life easier? It reduces the time required to complete routine tasks.

    How does it improve education? It expands access to learning resources. How does it affect employment?

    It can automate repetitive tasks and create new occupations. How does it affect communication? It removes geographical barriers.

    How does it create problems? It may reduce privacy and contribute to excessive screen time. Now your essay has substance.

    And that is what I want you to remember. Don’t just tell the examiner that technology is good or bad. Explain the mechanism. Technology does X.

    As a result, Y happens. This leads to Z. That’s how you develop an argument.

    One final thought

    Technology is strange. Every generation thinks the technology of its childhood was normal. But imagine explaining today’s world to someone from 100 years ago.

    You can instantly communicate with someone on another continent. You can carry a device in your pocket that contains more information than an enormous library. You can ask a machine to translate languages.

    You can learn from professors thousands of kilometers away. You can create images, music and videos with artificial intelligence. And yet, despite all of this technology, many of our basic problems remain.

    People still get lonely. People still worry about money. People still struggle to communicate.

    People still need meaningful relationships. People still need education. And people still have to decide how they want to live.

    Maybe that’s the most important point. Technology can change the tools available to us, but it doesn’t automatically tell us how those tools should be used. That’s still our responsibility.

    So, when you get a technology question in IELTS, don’t panic. You don’t need to know everything about artificial intelligence. You don’t need to be a computer scientist.

    You don’t need a huge vocabulary. You simply need to ask a few basic questions: What are the benefits?

    What are the drawbacks? Who benefits? Who might be disadvantaged?

    What should individuals do? What should governments do? And finally:

    How can we maximize the benefits while minimizing the risks? If you can think clearly about those questions, you already have the foundation for a very strong Task 2 essay. And once your ideas are clear, the language becomes much easier.

    Thanks for listening. And I’ll see you in the next episode.
